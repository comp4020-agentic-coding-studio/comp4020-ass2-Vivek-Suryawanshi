# Course Chain

## The idea

This course is one question chasing the next.

It does not contain twelve unrelated physics topics. Each week has to inherit a problem from the week before it, push that problem far enough to get a real answer, and then expose the next thing we cannot yet explain.

The chain is:

**Cosmos → heat → statistics → time → life → stars → quantum → matter → limits → cosmos**

The pattern is not:

> mystery → explanation → finished

It is:

> mystery → explanation → a better mystery

Physics should make the world easier to understand without making it less strange.

If two weeks can swap places and nothing breaks, the course has failed.

---

## Week 1 — Why is the night sky dark?

Look up at night.

Most of the sky is black.

That sounds ordinary until you imagine an infinite, eternal universe full of stars. In that universe, every direction you looked should eventually end on a star. The whole sky should blaze.

It does not.

So at least one of those assumptions is wrong.

The darkness between the stars is doing work. It carries information about the age and history of the universe.

The universe is not an eternal static backdrop. It has a history.

### What you need coming in

Nothing beyond curiosity and a willingness to question something that looks obvious.

### What changes this week

You stop treating darkness as "nothing."

An absence can be evidence.

### The question it leaves behind

If the universe has a history, what is the oldest light we can still see?

**Leads to:** Week 2
**Returns in:** Weeks 5 and 12

---

## Week 2 — What is the oldest light we can see?

The dark sky is not really dark.

Point the right instrument upward and the entire sky glows in microwaves.

That glow is ancient. It comes from a time when the early universe had cooled enough for light to travel freely instead of constantly scattering through hot matter.

Your eyes cannot see it, but it is everywhere.

Then comes the stranger part.

The spectrum is almost exactly the spectrum of something at a temperature.

The early universe looks thermal.

### What you need coming in

From Week 1: the universe has a history, and light can tell us about it.

### What changes this week

The empty-looking sky turns into a fossil record.

The universe has left light behind from its own early history.

### The question it leaves behind

What does it actually mean to say that the universe had a temperature?

**Leads to:** Week 3

---

## Week 3 — What is a temperature?

Put one molecule in an empty box.

Is it hot?

Not in the ordinary thermodynamic sense.

It has a speed. It has energy. But temperature is not the right description for one isolated particle.

Now put an enormous number of molecules in the box.

Suddenly temperature becomes one of the most reliable things you can measure.

Nothing new was added except more particles.

So where did the new property come from?

Temperature is a property of a statistical description. It belongs to the collective behaviour of many degrees of freedom.

### What you need coming in

From Week 2: the early universe can be described thermally.

### What changes this week

You stop assuming that every real property must already exist inside each individual part.

Some properties appear only when there are many parts.

### The question it leaves behind

How can a crowd have properties that none of its members have?

**Leads to:** Week 4

---

## Week 4 — How does a crowd have properties its members don't?

A crowd can be easier to predict than one member of it.

You cannot tell me exactly where one gas molecule will be a second from now.

Give me a litre of gas and I can predict its pressure extremely well.

That sounds backwards, but it is the whole point.

When the numbers become enormous, individual uncertainty can turn into collective reliability.

Pressure and temperature are not just scaled-up versions of one molecule's behaviour.

They are new ways of describing the crowd.

### What you need coming in

From Week 3: macroscopic properties can exist even when no single constituent possesses them.

### What changes this week

Statistics stops looking like a way of coping with ignorance.

It becomes part of the physics.

Large numbers can create stable behaviour from unpredictable pieces.

### The question it leaves behind

Now run the microscopic movie backwards.

The particles still obey perfectly legal physics.

So why does the macroscopic world seem to know which direction is forward in time?

**Leads to:** Week 5
**Returns in:** Week 10

---

## Week 5 — Why does time run one way?

Heat spreads out.

Perfume leaves the bottle and fills the room.

An egg breaks.

You do not expect any of these processes to reverse themselves.

But at the microscopic level, the basic rules often do not contain a built-in arrow saying:

**this way is the future.**

The arrow appears statistically.

Some macrostates can be realised by vastly more microstates than others. Systems tend toward the macrostates compatible with overwhelmingly more microscopic arrangements.

That is the heart of entropy here: counting, probability, and multiplicity.

Then the explanation turns around and causes trouble.

If entropy increases toward the future, the past must have been unusually ordered.

### What you need coming in

From Week 4: predictable macroscopic behaviour can emerge statistically from microscopic motion.

### What changes this week

Irreversibility stops looking like a separate law imposed on matter.

It becomes something that can emerge from probability.

### The question it leaves behind

If entropy overwhelmingly increases, how can organised structures persist at all?

**Leads to:** Week 6

**Cross-link:** Week 1.
The arrow of time depends on the universe having begun in an unusually special state.

---

## Week 6 — How do organised structures persist at all?

The second law does not say that every local thing has to become progressively featureless.

A whirlpool can persist while water flows through it.

A flame can keep its shape while fuel and oxygen pass through it.

A living organism can maintain extraordinary structure while constantly taking in energy and matter and dumping waste and heat back out.

Life is not a loophole in thermodynamics.

It works because it is not closed.

Organised structures can persist when energy flows through them.

On Earth, most of that usable energy ultimately traces back to one source.

The Sun.

### What you need coming in

From Week 5: entropy increase is about the statistical behaviour of whole systems, not a rule that forbids local structure.

### What changes this week

Order stops looking like a rebellion against thermodynamics.

It becomes something thermodynamics can allow when there is a sustained flow.

### The question it leaves behind

The Sun has been paying Earth's energy bill for billions of years.

Why has it not run out?

**Leads to:** Week 7

---

## Week 7 — Why hasn't the Sun burned out?

Now words stop being enough.

Suppose the Sun were burning chemically.

Calculate how long the available fuel could last.

The answer is far too short.

Suppose instead the Sun were slowly contracting under gravity and releasing energy.

Better.

Still far too short.

The Earth is billions of years old.

The Sun has clearly been shining for a comparable length of time.

You cannot settle this by arguing harder.

You have to calculate.

This is where serious mathematics enters the course because the question demands it.

The numbers force a new conclusion.

The Sun's energy is nuclear.

A small loss of mass corresponds to an enormous amount of energy.

But that creates the next problem immediately.

Fusion requires positively charged nuclei to get extremely close to one another.

They repel.

Classically, many of them should never get close enough.

### What you need coming in

From Week 6: life on Earth depends on a long-lived energy flow from the Sun.

### What changes this week

Calculation becomes a way to eliminate explanations.

Mathematics arrives because qualitative reasoning has reached its limit.

### The question it leaves behind

How do two nuclei that cannot classically get close enough manage to fuse?

**Leads to:** Week 8

---

## Week 8 — How do two nuclei that can't touch, touch?

Classically, some nuclei in the Sun do not have enough energy to cross the electrical barrier between them.

Yet fusion happens.

Quantum mechanics changes what "cannot" means.

A quantum state does not stop dead at the edge of a classically forbidden region.

It extends into it.

That makes tunnelling possible.

A process that should be impossible in a classical picture becomes possible in the real Sun.

So the Sun keeps shining because matter is not behaving like tiny classical balls following definite paths.

And now we have made the picture worse.

What exactly is a particle, then?

### What you need coming in

From Week 7: fusion must happen, even though classical reasoning says many nuclei should not get close enough.

### What changes this week

Classical impossibility becomes quantum probability.

A particle can have physical presence in places a classical trajectory would forbid.

### The question it leaves behind

If a particle is not simply a little object moving along a definite path, what is it?

**Leads to:** Week 9

---

## Week 9 — What is a particle?

Send particles through two slits.

Do it one at a time.

Each detection happens at one place.

But after many detections, an interference pattern appears.

A simple pellet picture cannot explain both facts at once.

The useful object in the theory is a quantum state.

It carries information about possible outcomes and their probabilities.

It can interfere.

But when you detect the particle, you do not find a fraction here and a fraction somewhere else.

You find one result.

One place.

One event.

### What you need coming in

From Week 8: matter can tunnel because quantum states do not behave like classical trajectories.

### What changes this week

"Particle" stops meaning "tiny hard object with a hidden path."

The state becomes more fundamental than the classical picture.

### The question it leaves behind

If the state spreads across possibilities, why does measurement give one definite result?

**Leads to:** Week 10

---

## Week 10 — What happens when you look?

Nothing magical happens because a human opens their eyes.

Measurement is a physical interaction.

A quantum system becomes entangled with an apparatus.

The apparatus becomes entangled with the surrounding environment.

And the environment is enormous.

That matters.

Interference between macroscopically different alternatives becomes effectively inaccessible through decoherence.

The world begins to look classical.

This is Week 4 returning in a new disguise.

One isolated microscopic system behaves one way.

A system tangled up with an enormous environment behaves differently.

But decoherence does not answer every foundational question about measurement.

Physics has explained a great deal here.

It has not closed the problem.

### What you need coming in

From Week 9: quantum states can contain multiple possible outcomes, while measurements give definite events.

### What changes this week

Measurement becomes a physical process instead of a mysterious act of "looking."

The environment becomes part of the explanation.

### The question it leaves behind

If the underlying world is quantum, why does the ordinary world look not only definite, but stable and solid?

**Leads to:** Week 11

**Cross-link:** Week 4.
Large collections and environments can behave in ways individual components do not.

---

## Week 11 — Why does matter behave like matter?

A table feels solid.

That does not mean its atoms are little hard spheres pressing directly against other hard spheres.

The real story is quantum and electromagnetic.

Atoms have stable quantum structures.

Electrons interact electromagnetically.

Identical fermions are constrained by the Pauli exclusion principle.

These effects together help give ordinary matter its structure, stability and resistance to compression.

Pauli exclusion matters enormously.

But "you do not fall through the floor because of Pauli exclusion" is too simple to be the whole explanation.

The details matter.

Then the same quantum rules reappear somewhere much less ordinary.

A dead star.

When nuclear fuel is gone, quantum effects can help hold stellar matter up against gravity.

But only up to a limit.

### What you need coming in

From Week 10: the macroscopic world has to emerge from underlying quantum behaviour.

### What changes this week

Solidity stops looking mechanical.

Stable matter turns out to be a quantum achievement.

### The question it leaves behind

What happens when gravity becomes too strong even for quantum matter to support itself?

**Leads to:** Week 12

---

## Week 12 — Where does explanation break?

Push a dead star hard enough and the support fails.

Now gravity takes over.

At extreme densities and gravitational fields, general relativity becomes essential.

At very small scales, quantum mechanics is essential.

And there are places where we need both.

That is where our current descriptions stop fitting together cleanly.

The problem is not that physics knows nothing.

The problem is that two extraordinarily successful theories do not yet form one complete description of those regimes.

Then zoom back out.

Most of the gravitating matter we infer in the universe has not been identified.

The accelerated expansion of the universe is described using dark energy, but its fundamental nature remains unknown.

The course ends where it began:

with the universe.

Except now the first question looks different.

### What you need coming in

From Week 11: quantum mechanics can stabilise matter, but only within limits.

### What changes this week

A theory can be extremely successful without being final.

The edge of physics is not where explanation disappears.

It is where explanations begin to conflict, run out, or point toward something missing.

### The question it leaves behind

Why does the universe have the history, contents and laws that made the dark sky of Week 1 possible?

Week 1 was never really closed.

# Cross-links

The course is a chain, but it is not just a straight line.

Some ideas disappear for several weeks and then return in a different form.

## Week 1 ↔ Week 5

Week 1 tells us the universe has a history.

Week 5 tells us the arrow of time depends on an unusually ordered past.

The beginning of the universe is therefore not just scenery.

It is connected to why broken eggs stay broken.

## Week 4 ↔ Week 10

Week 4 asks how enormous collections acquire behaviour that individual constituents do not have.

Week 10 asks a related question in quantum mechanics.

An isolated quantum system is one thing.

A quantum system entangled with an enormous environment is another.

The same idea returns at a deeper level.

## Week 1 ↔ Week 12

Week 1 begins with a dark sky and uses it to learn something about cosmic history.

Week 12 returns to the universe and finds that some of its largest features remain unexplained.

The semester closes a loop.

It does not close the question.

# The rule for every week

Every week has to answer two questions.

1. **What earlier idea does this week need?**
2. **What question does this week make unavoidable?**

If either answer is weak, the week does not belong.

A connection cannot be repaired with a sentence like:

> "This leads us to next week's topic."

The physics itself has to create the next question.

The student should reach the end of a week and feel that the next week is necessary.

---

# Mathematics

Do not introduce mathematics because this is a physics course.

Introduce mathematics when the physics forces the issue.

Weeks 1–6 can use numbers, estimates, graphs, comparisons and scales.

But Week 7 is the threshold.

The age of the Sun cannot be settled by verbal explanation.

Chemical burning sounds plausible until you calculate the lifetime.

Gravitational contraction sounds plausible until you calculate again.

The numbers kill both explanations.

That is why mathematics arrives there.

From Week 7 onward, equations can appear whenever they reveal something that words cannot.

The rule is:

> Start with the physical question. Let the question demand the mathematics.

# Voice

Write like someone is actually speaking to an intelligent student.

Start with the thing itself before naming the abstraction.

Stars before cosmology.

Hot objects before thermal spectra.

Gas molecules before statistical mechanics.

A burning Sun before nuclear physics.

Two slits before quantum states.

Use short sentences when the idea turns.

Do not make one-sentence paragraphs the default. They are for emphasis, interruption, reversal, or the moment an idea changes direction. Most explanatory prose should use ordinary paragraphs with several connected sentences.

Do not copy the line-break rhythm of this document mechanically. This file uses extra breaks because it is a design spec and the conceptual beats need to be visible. Lecture pages should read like prose, not like verse.

Say the surprising thing plainly.

Do not decorate the physics with fake wonder.

Avoid phrases like:

* "the important idea is"
* "this demonstrates"
* "it is useful to understand"
* "the deeper point is"
* "this highlights"
* "it is important to note"

If the point matters, make the point.

Do not tell the student that something is fascinating.

Make it fascinating.

Technical words are welcome when the student has reached the problem that needs them.

Do not use jargon as atmosphere.

Where a popular explanation is too neat, say so.

Where the physics is settled, explain it clearly.

Where the physics is not settled, do not pretend otherwise.

The course should never confuse mystery with vagueness.

Its job is to explain as much as physics genuinely can — and then show exactly what remains.
