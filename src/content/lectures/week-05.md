Perfume released from an open bottle fills the room. It does not later gather itself back into the bottle. Heat flows from a hot object into a cooler one until both reach the same temperature; a lukewarm object does not spontaneously separate into a hot half and a cold half. Drop an egg and it breaks; the pieces do not reassemble themselves and jump back into your hand.

You have never seen any of these things run in reverse, and you would not believe your eyes if you did.

Here is the trouble. Look at the equations that describe individual collisions, individual molecules, individual bounces — the mechanics underneath all three examples — and, mostly, they do not care which way time runs. Reverse every velocity in a system of colliding particles and you get another valid sequence of events, equally legal under the same rules. Nothing in the microscopic description says "this direction is the future."

So where does the arrow come from?

Go back to counting. A gas confined to one corner of a room can be arranged in a certain number of ways at the microscopic level — this molecule here, that one there, each with some particular speed and direction. A gas spread evenly through the whole room can be arranged in a vastly larger number of ways. Not a little larger. Vastly larger, by a margin that gets more extreme the more molecules you add.

Nothing forces the gas to spread out. It is still true that, in principle, every molecule could happen to drift back into one corner at the same moment, and the underlying mechanics would not object. But among all the ways the room's molecules could possibly be arranged, arrangements that look like "gas filling the room evenly" enormously outnumber arrangements that look like "gas crammed into one corner." Left alone, a system tends to end up looking like whichever arrangement has an overwhelming majority of ways to happen, simply because that is what an enormous number of essentially random rearrangements will produce almost every time. That count of available arrangements is what entropy tracks.

This is why the perfume spreads and the egg does not unbreak. Not because reversal is forbidden by the underlying physics, but because the reversed version requires the system to sit in one of an almost unimaginably small number of special arrangements, and nothing is steering it there.

So the arrow of time is not a separate law bolted onto mechanics from outside. It is closer to a matter of overwhelming likelihood, working on a system with an enormous number of moving parts.

That explanation immediately turns around and causes a new kind of trouble. Look back through the actual history of the universe and you find the opposite of a typical, high-entropy past. Trace entropy backward far enough, past every egg and every perfume bottle, past the Earth, past the stars, and it keeps getting lower, all the way back to a hot, dense, remarkably uniform early universe of a kind Week 1 and Week 2 already put in front of you. An early universe that low in entropy is, by any reasonable counting argument, spectacularly unlikely.

Physics can explain why entropy rises from a given starting point. It has a much harder time explaining why the universe started at a point so low.

That is unfinished business for later in the course. For now, take entropy's rise as given, and ask what it permits rather than what it forbids. An overwhelming statistical tendency toward disorder does not mean every corner of the universe is required to become featureless. It leaves open a narrower and much stranger question.

## What we explained

Time appears to run one way not because the underlying mechanics forbid reversal, but because the arrangements we call "ordinary" — spread-out gas, mixed cream, a broken egg — can be realised by an overwhelmingly larger number of microscopic configurations than the arrangements we call "special." Left alone, a system with enough moving parts drifts toward whichever outcome has that overwhelming statistical majority, and that drift is what entropy measures.
