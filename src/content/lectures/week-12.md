Last week left a dead star held up by its own electrons, packed so tightly that quantum exclusion forces them apart hard enough to resist gravity. It also left a number: add mass past about 1.4 times the Sun's, and no arrangement of electrons can supply enough pressure to hold the star up.

Push past that limit and see what happens.

## A mechanism running out

Nothing mysterious happens first. Gravity simply wins. The electrons themselves get forced to combine with protons, turning much of the star into neutrons, and the collapse continues until a new kind of pressure takes over: neutrons resisting exclusion the same way electrons did, packed even more tightly. That gives you a neutron star, and it works, for a while, up to a mass limit of its own — somewhere around two to three times the Sun's mass, though the exact number is still being pinned down.

Push past that second limit and there is no third mechanism waiting. No known form of pressure, no known particle, no known force can generate enough outward push to stop the collapse. Gravity wins outright, and keeps winning. This is a mechanism failing at a boundary we can calculate, not a gap in the physics that produced it. We understand exclusion pressure, we understand gravity, and we understand exactly why one eventually loses to the other. What happens after that — at the very centre of what has now become a black hole — is a different kind of problem entirely.

## Two good theories that will not fit together

General relativity describes gravity superbly, as curved spacetime, and it works from the scale of planets up to the scale of galaxies. Quantum mechanics describes particles and fields superbly, and it works from atoms down to whatever is smaller than that. Each theory is, on its own ground, about as well tested as a scientific theory gets.

Neither one was built to handle a region where an entire star's worth of mass is compressed into something small enough that quantum effects and gravitational curvature both matter at once — the centre of a black hole, or the universe in its first instant. Try to apply general relativity there and it predicts a singularity, a point of infinite density, which usually means a theory has been pushed past where it applies rather than that infinity is physically real. Try to bring in quantum mechanics to fix that, and there is no agreed way to combine it with curved spacetime in that regime. Both theories keep working perfectly everywhere they have been tested. They simply do not join up where you need them most.

That is not the same failure as a support mechanism running out. Nothing here is losing a battle against gravity. Two correct, successful descriptions of nature run out of shared ground.

## Things we have not identified at all

Zoom out from a single collapsing star to the whole universe, and a third kind of gap shows up — not a mechanism reaching its limit, not two theories clashing, but something physics has not yet been able to name.

Galaxies rotate, and light bends around massive clusters, in ways that only make sense if there is far more gravitating mass out there than anything we can see or detect directly. Add it up and it dwarfs ordinary matter: something like five parts in a hundred of the universe's mass and energy is the matter that makes up stars, planets, and everything discussed so far this semester. Most of the rest is inferred entirely from its gravity and has never been directly identified. Separately, the universe's expansion is not just continuing, it is speeding up, and the name given to whatever is driving that is dark energy — a label for the effect, not an explanation of its cause.

This is the strangest of the three, because it is not a boundary condition or an incompatibility. It is an admission that most of what the universe is made of and most of what is pushing it apart remains, so far, unidentified.

## Back to the dark sky

Week 1 asked why the night sky is dark, and the answer turned on the universe having a finite age and a history — a beginning, an expansion, a moment after which light could finally travel freely. That history is exactly what the rest of this course has been filling in: heat, statistics, time's arrow, stars, nuclei, quantum states, measurement, matter.

But the account of that history was never complete, and it still isn't. It depends on contents we can weigh but not identify, on a process of accelerated expansion we can measure but not explain, and on physical laws that we cannot yet write down for the universe's own earliest moment, when gravity and quantum mechanics both had to matter and neither theory alone was equipped to say what happened. The dark sky was real evidence of a real history. It was never the same thing as a finished explanation of that history, and it still isn't.

## What we explained

Explanation breaks in at least three distinct ways. A support mechanism can fail at a calculable limit, as when gravity overwhelms a dead star's exclusion pressure, and there is nothing unknown about why it fails. Two successful theories, general relativity and quantum mechanics, can each work perfectly within their own domain and still have no agreed way to combine in the most extreme regimes, such as a black hole's centre. And some of the universe's basic contents and driving processes — most of its gravitating matter, and whatever is accelerating its expansion — have not been identified at all. These are not the same kind of gap, and treating them as one undifferentiated pile of "unsolved problems" would hide what is actually going on in each case.
