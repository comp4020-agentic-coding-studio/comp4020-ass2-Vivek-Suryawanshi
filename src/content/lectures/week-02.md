Point a radio receiver at the sky, at any patch of it, and it is not silent. There is a faint hiss arriving from every direction, day or night, whether you point at a galaxy or at the blackest gap between two of them.

That hiss is light. Two radio astronomers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, spent the best part of a year in 1964 trying to get rid of this hiss. They cleaned pigeon droppings out of their antenna. The noise stayed. It turned out they were listening to the universe. You cannot see it because your eyes only catch a narrow slice of the electromagnetic spectrum, and this light sits well outside that slice, out in the microwave band. But it is there, arriving from every direction with almost exactly the same intensity.

Last week the dark sky told us the universe has a history. It has not been shining forever, so most lines of sight can end on nothing. This week's glow is a piece of that history arriving directly. It is old light, released from a time when the universe was hot and dense enough that matter and light could not separate. Photons scattered constantly off free electrons, unable to travel far before being knocked into a new direction. There was no clear line of sight anywhere.

Then the universe cooled. Electrons and nuclei combined into neutral atoms, photons stopped scattering off free charges, and light was finally free to travel in a straight line. The photons released at that moment are still travelling. Some of them just reached your receiver.

That would already be strange enough. Here is the part that makes it stranger.

Measure the glow's spectrum — how much power arrives at each wavelength — and it is not some ragged leftover shape. It sits almost exactly on the curve a hot object in thermal equilibrium would radiate: a blackbody spectrum, peaked at one wavelength and falling away smoothly on either side of it.

The early universe did not just leave behind radiation. It left behind radiation carrying the signature of a specific temperature, as if the whole universe, at the moment light stopped scattering, could be assigned one thermodynamic number.

That should stop you. Temperature is a property you learn about in a lab, attached to a cup of coffee or a box of gas sitting on a bench. Now it is being attached to the entire universe, at an age with no lab, no coffee, and no single object in the ordinary sense to call hot.

So what is actually being measured?

## What we explained

The empty-looking sky is not empty. It glows in microwaves with ancient light, released when the universe first cooled enough for photons to travel freely instead of scattering endlessly through hot matter. That glow's spectrum matches the shape radiated by a system in thermal equilibrium, which is why the early universe can be described as having had a temperature.
